# Critical Literature Review as Explanatory Diagnosis

Use this guide to build Related Work and theoretical positioning from sources the researcher has supplied or explicitly asked to locate and verify. The workflow moves from source-level diagnosis to a corpus-level explanation of why the present study is needed.

## Begin with the review's communicative purpose

State four items before building the review:

1. the phenomenon and explanatory problem;
2. the target reader and the question this reader needs answered;
3. the main message the review should establish;
4. the decision, interpretation, or research direction that message should change.

Build a hierarchy of focus questions. Include descriptive questions about what prior work studied, analytical questions about how its explanations work, and evaluative questions about where those explanations lose reach. Put the questions in the order a target reader needs them. Use [audience-question-outline.md](../assets/audience-question-outline.md) when the review or full paper needs explicit planning or a reverse outline.

## Diagnose each source at the level of explanation

Use one row per source in [critical-literature-matrix.csv](../assets/critical-literature-matrix.csv). Record the source's object of explanation, material, finding, required premise, boundary, unobserved step, anomaly, stated limitation, and the researcher's reading of the limitation.

Keep these distinctions visible:

- **Material and setting:** what the study actually examined, with method, participants or documents, time frame, and context.
- **Finding established:** what the evidence supports at the source's own level.
- **Required premise:** the condition that must hold for the source's explanation to work.
- **Boundary condition:** the point where that premise stops holding.
- **Asserted and unobserved:** a process, mechanism, actor judgment, or transition that the explanation assumes without examining.
- **Anomaly or residue:** a reported result that the source's framework leaves unresolved.
- **Author-stated limitation:** the boundary the source explicitly identifies.
- **Researcher-inferred limitation:** the explanatory limit established through close reading and comparison.

The difference between the final two fields often reveals a productive research position. Treat this difference as an interpretation that needs evidence and a serious counterargument.

## Classify the explanatory failure

Use one primary label and add a secondary label only when the distinction changes the synthesis:

1. **Scope premise fails:** an explanation developed in one set of conditions is applied where those conditions no longer hold.
2. **Mechanism asserted without observation:** the source moves from input to outcome while leaving a consequential step unexamined.
3. **Material and claim mismatch:** the evidence type supports a narrower claim than the source makes.
4. **Construct loses coherence:** one term combines several forms of work, relation, or experience that require separate explanation.
5. **Time scale mismatch:** a snapshot supports a claim about an ongoing process, adaptation, or change.
6. **Unresolved conflict:** credible studies reach incompatible conclusions and the field lacks an explanation for the difference.
7. **Framework cannot explain a reported anomaly:** the source reports a result that its own concepts cannot absorb.

Write the failure as a causal or interpretive sentence:

> Existing explanations require [premise]. In [relevant condition], the premise fails because [evidence]. As a result, they leave [phenomenon, process, or relation] unexplained.

A statement that research has not examined a topic identifies an opening. It becomes a defensible gap when the missing work changes an explanation, decision, or research direction.

## Build a gap across the corpus

A gap belongs to a set of sources. Assign the same `gap_id` to sources that expose the same explanatory failure. Treat a gap supported by one source as a provisional lead. Promote it into the paper's positioning after comparison shows the failure across at least two relevant sources or a coherent body of work.

For every retained `gap_id`:

1. state the strongest existing explanation;
2. identify the shared premise or omitted step;
3. compare material, context, and claim scope across the supporting sources;
4. record the strongest counterargument and the literature that could defeat the gap;
5. explain the consequence of leaving the failure unresolved;
6. mark whether the present study's data provide full, partial, or no purchase on the gap;
7. define the claim the study can establish and the claim it must leave open.

Discard or narrow a gap when the counterargument succeeds, the source comparison fails, or the study's data cannot address the explanatory problem.

## Map conversations after source diagnosis

Group the matrix by scholarly problem and explanation. For each conversation, synthesise:

- central question and object of explanation;
- strongest explanation and shared premises;
- material, settings, and methods supporting it;
- points of agreement, conflict, and residue;
- conditions under which the explanation retains or loses reach;
- role in the present paper.

Useful roles include empirical baseline, conceptual tool, methodological warrant, dialogue partner, competing explanation, boundary case, and bridge between literatures. Use [literature-dialogue-map.md](../assets/literature-dialogue-map.md) for this corpus-level synthesis.

## Construct the positioning argument

Develop the Related Work argument through this sequence:

1. Establish the consequential phenomenon and the conversation the paper enters.
2. Present the strongest current explanation and the evidence supporting it.
3. Show the repeated premise failure, omitted mechanism, unresolved conflict, or anomaly.
4. Explain why this explanatory failure matters to HCI understanding, design, practice, or governance.
5. State what the present material and analysis can establish.
6. Define the scope of that intervention and the evidence still needed.

An unstudied setting gains significance when it exposes a consequential premise, mechanism, or decision problem. The importance argument belongs beside the gap.

## Draft through questions and comparative claims

Give each section one target-reader question and one answer. Give each paragraph one comparative or argumentative job. Use citations to establish patterns, tensions, lineages, premises, and limits. A sequence of paper summaries does not perform synthesis.

For each paragraph, check:

- **Point:** the answer or subclaim;
- **Evidence:** the relevant sources and their material;
- **Explanation:** why the evidence supports the point and how sources relate;
- **Link:** how the point advances the section question and the paper's positioning.

Create a reverse outline from a draft by recording the actual question and answer of every paragraph. Revise when adjacent paragraphs answer unrelated questions, a paragraph supplies information without a point, or the section title gives only a broad topic.

## HCI cross-tradition bridge

When combining HCI, CSCW, organisational studies, STS, information systems, or humanities theory:

- preserve each tradition's unit of analysis and concept meaning;
- identify conceptual compatibility, complementarity, and tension;
- explain the cost of moving a concept into a new empirical or disciplinary setting;
- specify which tradition supplies description, mechanism, norm, or design relevance;
- keep the HCI contribution visible after the bridge is built.

## Verification boundary

Use original texts for load-bearing claims and quotations. Give each matrix row a verification state such as `not checked`, `metadata checked`, `text checked`, `claim supported`, `partly supported`, or `contradicted`. Mark remembered, secondary, or unverified descriptions with `[VERIFY]`. Never attribute a position to an author from memory.

Quick capture can begin with source ID, citation, material, and verification state. Leave analytical cells empty until the source has been read closely. Example rows and invented content must never serve as evidence.
