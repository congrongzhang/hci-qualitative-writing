# Authorial Voice and Target Reader

Use this guide when drafting, revising, translating, or checking whether prose still belongs to the researcher.

## Two-part model

Treat voice and audience as connected profiles:

- The **voice profile** records how this researcher develops and expresses scholarly judgment.
- The **reader profile** records the knowledge, expectations, and likely misunderstandings of the intended audience.

Use both profiles when they exist. For a one-off passage, infer only what the supplied material supports and label the inference as provisional.

## Build the voice profile from evidence

Prefer two or three author-approved samples that represent the desired voice. Exclude unreviewed AI drafts. Record examples alongside every claimed pattern.

Analyse:

- argumentative rhythm, including claim-first, evidence-first, or iterative development;
- scholarly posture toward prior work, disagreement, participants, and affected groups;
- first-person and collective-person practice;
- preferred terminology and stable translations;
- sentence length, cadence, paragraph openings, and transition style;
- the rhetorical function of citations, including support, contrast, concept introduction, methodological warrant, and dialogue;
- preferred strength of claims and forms of qualification;
- section-specific changes in voice across Methods, Findings, and Discussion;
- expressions the author has explicitly rejected.

Separate productive voice from accidental errors. Preserve intentional long sentences, abrupt pivots, or first-person judgments when they carry analytical meaning. Correct ambiguity, unsupported claims, and broken logic even when they recur in a sample.

## Learn from revision choices

The strongest signals come from the researcher's response to revisions:

- accepted suggestion: confirms direction;
- rejected suggestion with a reason: identifies a boundary or preference;
- author-modified suggestion: reveals a more precise voice pattern;
- repeated terminology correction: establishes a fixed rendering;
- conscious style change: records an evolution, not an error.

Append changes to the profile with date, evidence, and context. Keep previous entries when they explain the development of the current rule.

## Build the reader profile

Specify the primary reader and any secondary reader. For each, record:

- disciplinary and methodological expertise;
- familiarity with the empirical setting and theoretical tradition;
- contribution type they are likely to value;
- evidence and transparency expectations;
- concepts that require definition;
- assumptions they may contest;
- likely misreadings of the paper's scope, method, or contribution;
- questions they need answered and the order in which they need them;
- the judgment or action the paper should enable;
- criteria they will use to assess the research response;
- information they need repeated at the point of interpretation;
- vocabulary and framing that signal fit with the community.

For CHI or CSCW, map the reader to the actual subcommittee, track, research paradigm, and current review criteria when available.

## Revision test

Before returning revised prose, check:

1. Does the argument remain the researcher's argument?
2. Does the sequence of thought match the author's established rhythm?
3. Are fixed terms and first-person practices preserved?
4. Does every change improve accuracy, logic, audience access, or style consistency?
5. Can the intended reader follow the tacit premises and evidence steps?
6. Has adaptation to the reader changed the claim's meaning or strength?
7. Do headings and paragraph openings help the reader identify the question and message without flattening the author's rhythm?
8. Does the draft remove detail that distracts this reader while preserving evidence needed for assessment?

If a clean rewrite would erase a meaningful voice feature, provide a lighter revision and explain the choice.
